Attention mechanisms have undergone a profound evolution from auxiliary alignment components to the foundational architectural principle of modern sequence models. This transformation began with Bahdanau et al.'s seminal work on neural machine translation, which introduced a soft alignment mechanism that enabled encoder-decoder RNNs to dynamically focus on relevant source positions when generating each target token [Bahdanau et al.]. Unlike hard alignment approaches, this differentiable attention computed weighted averages of encoder hidden states, where weights were learned through a feedforward network that scored compatibility between the current decoder state and all encoder states. While revolutionary for its time, this approach remained constrained by the sequential nature of RNNs, limiting parallelization and making it difficult to capture long-range dependencies due to the vanishing gradient problem.

The Transformer architecture marked a paradigm shift by dispensing with recurrence entirely and building a model solely on attention mechanisms [Attention]. This design eliminated the sequential bottleneck of RNNs, enabling full parallelization during training and dramatically reducing training time. At its core, the Transformer introduced scaled dot-product attention, which computes attention scores as the dot product of query and key vectors, scaled by the square root of the key dimension to prevent softmax saturation in high-dimensional spaces. This formulation is computationally efficient and provides a natural measure of similarity between representations.

To enhance the model's capacity to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions, the Transformer employs multi-head attention. By linearly projecting queries, keys, and values into multiple smaller subspaces and applying attention independently in each, the model can learn distinct patterns of relationships—such as syntactic, semantic, and coreferential dependencies—within a single layer. This parallelism significantly improves representational power over single-head attention, allowing the model to capture richer contextual relationships.

Since self-attention is permutation-invariant and cannot inherently distinguish sequence order, the Transformer incorporates positional encodings—fixed or learned vectors added to input embeddings to inject information about token position. The original implementation used sinusoidal functions of different frequencies to encode positions, ensuring the model could extrapolate to sequences longer than those seen during training. This design elegantly addresses the fundamental limitation of attention-only architectures while maintaining the benefits of parallel computation and global context access.

Collectively, these innovations transformed attention from a soft alignment mechanism that supplemented RNN-based architectures into a fully learnable, self-referential, and architecture-defining component. The Transformer's ability to draw global dependencies between any two positions in constant time—unlike RNNs (linear) or CNNs (logarithmic or linear)—represents a fundamental functional improvement. Moreover, the representational capacity of multi-head self-attention enables the model to simultaneously capture diverse linguistic phenomena across multiple abstraction levels, establishing a new state of the art in translation quality and generalizing effectively to other sequence tasks.
