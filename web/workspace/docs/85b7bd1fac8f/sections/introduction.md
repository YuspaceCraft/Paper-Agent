Remote sensing is the science and technology of acquiring information about objects or areas from a distance—typically from aircraft or satellites—without making physical contact. It relies on detecting and measuring electromagnetic radiation reflected or emitted by the Earth's surface and atmosphere across various spectral bands, from visible light through infrared to microwave frequencies. This capability enables systematic, repeatable, and synoptic observation of our planet at spatial scales ranging from local to global.

The significance of remote sensing in Earth observation and environmental monitoring is profound and multifaceted. It provides critical, objective data for tracking dynamic Earth system processes that are otherwise difficult or impossible to monitor comprehensively through ground-based methods alone. Remote sensing supports long-term environmental change detection, facilitates near-real-time situational awareness, and underpins evidence-based decision-making for sustainable resource management and policy development.

Major application domains demonstrate the field's broad societal impact. In agriculture, remote sensing enables precision farming practices through crop health assessment, yield prediction, and irrigation optimization. For disaster response, it delivers rapid mapping of affected areas, damage assessment, and support for emergency planning before, during, and after events such as floods, wildfires, and earthquakes. In climate science, remote sensing provides essential observational records for monitoring key climate variables—including sea surface temperature, ice sheet mass balance, atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations, and land cover change—that are fundamental to understanding climate dynamics and validating climate models.

This review aims to provide a structured synthesis of the remote sensing discipline, with particular emphasis on three interconnected dimensions: (1) foundational concepts that define the physical principles, sensor types, and data processing methodologies; (2) the technological evolution that has driven advances in spatial, spectral, temporal, and radiometric resolution over decades; and (3) current challenges—including data volume and complexity, algorithmic limitations, integration with in-situ measurements, and emerging ethical and governance considerations—that shape the future trajectory of the field.
